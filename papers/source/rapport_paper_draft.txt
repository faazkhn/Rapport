
# Rapport paper draft
2026-10-25 · Faaz
Working title: Rapport: an embodied, on-device psychiatric standardised patient driven by a research-grounded Digital Twin
## Abstract (draft)
Psychiatric OSCE training depends on standardised patients who can portray guarded, slowly unfolding presentations, but trained actors are scarce and inconsistent, and current LLM patient simulators are text-only, cloud-hosted and stateless about trust and mood. We present Rapport, a voice-driven 3D standardised patient that runs fully on a single laptop. Its behaviour is set by a Digital Twin: an explicit, inspectable model of the patient's formulation, trust-gated memory, appraisal, mood and behaviour choice, synthesised from published LLM patient simulators and framed by the computational theories of the Brain/MINDS 2.0 Digital Brain programme. The language model voices the Twin's decisions in a single structured call; physical examination requests are routed by rules (385 test utterances, 100% exact, 0 false triggers); an examiner-facing view shows the Twin's state live, also to a second device on the local network. Four draft stations (moderate depression, two acute-ward scenarios, alcohol dependence with withdrawal) run on four rigged bodies, with seeded patients and six conversation styles. On an Apple M5 Pro with Gemma 4 E4B and an emotional text-designed voice (Qwen3-TTS), median doctor-stop-to-patient-audio latency is 2.2 s over 26 turns; a playback hold removes mid-sentence audio breaks when speech synthesis and the language model share the GPU. A held-out suite of 12 scripted conversations (98 turns) found 0 invented facts after hand review, 100% valid output and correct risk disclosure. The debrief scores the learner against what the patient actually showed and said, with MIRS-style communication items, disclosure-based risk scoring, a global rating and quote-grounded feedback. [PENDING: fidelity against PatientHub baselines, faculty blind A/B realism, learner pilot.] We release the case schema and evaluation suite to support reproducible psychiatric patient simulation.
## 1. Introduction
The psychiatric interview is learned by doing: a trainee must notice slowed speech and averted gaze, ask open questions, earn enough trust for a patient to disclose passive death wishes, and assess risk, all within a timed OSCE station. Standardised patient actors make this possible but are costly, unevenly available and hard to keep consistent across examinees ([https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.658967] Piot et al. 2021).
LLM-based patient simulators have grown quickly ([https://github.com/FreedomIntelligence/Awesome-LLM-Patient-Simulators] Awesome-LLM-Patient-Simulators), and psychiatric ones now model cognitive formulations, resistance and trust-gated disclosure (PATIENT-Ψ, PSYCHE, PatientAct). Three gaps remain for OSCE use:
Embodiment. Most simulators are text chat. Psychiatric assessment depends on speech rate, latency, affect and posture, which text cannot carry.
Real-time constraint. Rich patient agents make several LLM calls per turn (about 6 in PatientAct), which is too slow for spoken dialogue on local hardware.
Inspectability and privacy. Examiners cannot see why a simulated patient disclosed or withheld, and cloud-hosted models send learner audio off-site.
Contributions (draft; final list depends on results):
C1. Rapport, an open, on-device, voice-driven 3D standardised patient for psychiatry and addiction OSCEs: four draft stations, four rigged bodies, seeded patients with six conversation styles.
C2. The Digital Twin, an explicit patient-mind model (formulation, trust-gated memory, appraisal, predictive trust update, behaviour choice, mood) that unifies components from published simulators and is framed by the computational theories of the Brain/MINDS 2.0 Digital Brain programme.
C3. A fast-path and slow-path split that keeps one structured LLM call on the critical path, with measured end-to-end latency, and a playback hold that keeps streamed speech unbroken on a shared GPU.
C4. An examiner-facing live view of the Twin's state, served to a second device on the local network.
C5. Embodied physical examination: a rules-based exam router (385 test utterances) driving hand presentation with forearm twist, rolled sleeves and forearm inspection, tremor, gaze and gait.
C6. A literature-grounded learner assessment: MSE scored against the signs the patient actually showed, disclosure-based risk scoring with case-critical items, MIRS-style communication items, a global rating, and quote-grounded feedback with optional AI examiner notes checked against the transcript.
C7. An evaluation suite and release gate covering invented facts, jailbreaks, portrayal fidelity and safety, and a gated learning loop that rejected an unsafe learned version. [PENDING: baseline comparison]
## 2. Related work
LLM psychiatric patient simulators. Chen et al. ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13614] 2023) built clinician-validated ChatGPT patients for depression interviews. PATIENT-Ψ ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19660] Wang et al. 2024) grounds patients in 106 expert-made CBT cognitive models with six conversation styles. PSYCHE ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01594] Lee et al. 2025) builds an MSE-based construct to evaluate psychiatric assessment agents. PatientAct ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12750] 2026) adds trust-gated memory, reactions, behaviours and seven resistance patterns; PatientHub ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11684] 2026) packages 16 simulators behind one interface. Roleplay-doh ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00870] Louie et al. 2024) lets experts steer a patient through written principles. Rapport adopts these components but fuses them into one structured call and makes the state visible.
Learning simulators. EvoPatient ([https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.846/] ACL 2025) improves standardised patients through doctor-patient coevolution and a vetted experience library; MedAgentSim ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.22678] 2025) and Agent Hospital ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02957] 2024) evolve agents in simulated clinics. We restrict learning to wording and behaviour, never clinical facts, and gate every change on a regression suite.
Embodied and voice virtual patients. Embodied virtual patients for psychiatric communication predate LLMs (design guidelines, [https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12193-020-00338-8] 2020). Recent LLM-driven 3D patients include Voigt et al. ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13943] 2025), where the LLM selects actions a 3D patient performs, and SOPHIE ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02694] 2025) for serious-illness conversations. SimPatient ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18673] 2025) and MedSimAI ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05793] 2025) add formative feedback. To our knowledge none combines a psychiatric patient-mind model, sub-two-second local voice and a live examiner view. [VERIFY with a systematic search before submission.]
Digital brains and computational psychiatry. Brain/MINDS 2.0 Group 4 ([https://brainminds.jp/en/research/222] Development of the Digital Brain) builds data-driven brain models and tests whether reinforcement learning, Bayesian inference, predictive coding and active inference emerge from them; its subproject 4F models neuropsychiatric disease from patient data. Rapport's Twin is not a neural model; it borrows these theories as behavioural mechanisms (for example, trust as a prediction-error update). [ADD: computational psychiatry references on predictive processing accounts of depression.]
Evaluation. LLM-as-judge methods ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685] Zheng et al. 2023) scale but are biased; we pair them with faculty blind ratings. An RCT ([https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11605890/] 2024) shows LLM patient simulation with structured feedback improves students' clinical decisions, and learners rate actionable feedback far above avatar realism (4.7 vs 3.4 of 5 in [https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02694] SOPHIE). On learner assessment, LLMs scoring OSCE transcripts on the Master Interview Rating Scale match expert scores exactly only 27-44% of the time but within one point 67-87%, are weakest on jargon, acknowledging impact and non-verbal items, and do best zero-shot ([https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13957] Geathers et al. 2025); MedSimAI reports 87% agreement at the pass/fail threshold. A 2026 meta-analysis of AI virtual patients for psychiatric interviews (10 studies, about 450 participants) finds gains in confidence and knowledge but poorly standardised outcomes ([https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2026.1834636/full] Kilincel et al. 2026). Clinically, risk should be assessed rather than stratified ([https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7615272/] NICE NG225), and OSCEs pair checklists with a global rating whose pass mark is set by borderline regression on a cohort ([https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s12909-018-1421-x] BMC Med Educ 2018). Rapport's debrief follows these findings (section 3.7).
## 3. System
### 3.1 Architecture
Rapport has three processes on one laptop (Apple M5 Pro, 24 GB in our tests): a Unity 6 client (3D room, patient, UI, mic capture, lip sync), a Python patient service (WebSocket; VAD with Silero plus Smart Turn v3 endpointing, speculative STT with Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2 via parakeet-mlx, the Twin, a rules-based exam router, TTS with Qwen3-TTS 1.7B VoiceDesign via mlx-audio and KittenTTS as fallback), and llama.cpp serving Gemma 4 E4B (Q4_K_M) with a JSON grammar and a no-thinking chat template. No audio or text leaves the device; only the read-only Twin view is served to the local network, so a second laptop can show it to examiners.
### 3.2 Case representation
A case (schema v0.3) holds identity and body/voice variants; clinical facts each with a trust threshold, activation tags and the intents that unlock them; per-domain MSE behaviour with example lines; starting state (trust, distress, agitation, engagement) and drift; cues with gains on state and time; exam findings with trust-gated actions; rating-scale item scores (PHQ-9 and GAD-7, or AUDIT and CIWA-Ar); banned clinical words; a 5Ps formulation, core belief and attachment style; and a hidden answer key (diagnosis, differentials, required and case-critical intents, safe and unsafe prescriptions, required disposition, expected MSE). Clinically neutral slots (names, hometown, hobbies) and one of six conversation styles can be drawn from a seed while the answer key stays fixed; ward stations add scripted events whose timing code decides. Cases carry author and reviewer fields, and release builds refuse unverified cases. Four draft cases exist: moderate depression (mdd_01), two acute-ward stations (ward_01 wants to go home, ward_02 refuses medication) and alcohol dependence with early withdrawal and a past withdrawal seizure (alc_01).
### 3.3 The Digital Twin
Each turn, the Twin computes, in order:
Recall (no LLM, about 10 ms): match the doctor's words to memory items by activation tags and similarity; drop items above the current trust; a locked match emits only a discomfort signal.
Appraise and choose (one structured LLM call): the behaviour and emotion labels are generated first, then the utterance, then reaction, intensity, resistance pattern and state deltas, so the face and the voice's emotional instruction are set before speech streams to TTS (all-labels-first measured 3.1 s median latency against 1.6 s).
Express: labels map to face presets, gaze, tremor, posture, TTS speed and a deliberate pause for psychomotor slowing.
Update (slow path, off the critical path): trust moves by a bounded step reflecting the gap between the patient's expectation and the doctor's act (empathy raises, judgement lowers), clamped in code; mood drifts on pleasure, arousal and dominance; the episode is logged.

### 3.4 Latency engineering
Five changes cut median lag from 3.0 s to 1.6 s with KittenTTS: the deliberate pause now absorbs compute; a no-thinking template removed stray reasoning that blocked the JSON grammar; bounded schema fields; next-turn prompt prefill while the doctor is speaking (Gemma's sliding-window cache cannot reuse a rewritten facts block); and VAD silence reduced to 500 ms. A main-thread await bug that let mic latency grow over a session was also fixed.
The emotional voice and richer realism rules then cost about 0.6 s: with Qwen3-TTS, speculative STT, Smart Turn endpointing and the per-turn context moved before the doctor's words, median lag is 2.2 s (p90 4.0 s). Qwen3-TTS synthesises at 0.53 times real time alone but at about 1.0 while the language model generates on the same GPU, so audio streamed from the first chunk ran dry mid-word. The service now holds each sentence until the audio buffered ahead outlasts the rest of its synthesis, estimated from the measured rate. In simulation at 1.05-1.2 times real time this removed all 11-12 breaks per reply, at no cost on an idle GPU and about 1 s on an overloaded one.
### 3.5 Examiner view
A browser view shows the Twin's state live: gauges, a mood plot, a 3D brain metaphor of the reply's components, the memory ladder unlocking as trust rises, the 5Ps formulation, and a one-line reason for each reply. It receives a per-turn snapshot over a WebSocket, catches up when opened mid-session, logs each session for replay, and can be opened from a second device on the same network; every other endpoint answers only the host.
### 3.6 Embodied physical examination
Requests such as "hold out your hands", "roll up your sleeves" or "stand up and walk" are routed by rules, not the model (385 utterances written before the router: 100% exact actions and slots, 0% false triggers on 56 negatives, 0.01 ms per call; optimistic because one author wrote both). Trust and agitation gate compliance, and the patient refuses in words below the case's threshold. Actions queue in order, hold until "relax" or 30 s, and drive humanoid IK. Hand presentation turns the palm with the forearm twist bones carrying the roll, so a palms-up request no longer twists the wrist; shirt sleeves roll up through blendshapes generated at runtime from skin weights, so any rigged body works; held hands show the case's tremor (fine at about 10 Hz, or coarse at 5-6 Hz in withdrawal), and sweating shows as skin sheen. Four bodies (two per sex) share one skeleton and set of face shapes; one carries the 52 ARKit expression shapes for future facial capture.
### 3.7 Learner assessment and debrief
The debrief is scored in code from what happened in the session, risk first (risk 30, observation 20, history 12, communication 12, diagnosis 13, management 13):
Risk: each risk item, plus the case's critical items (for example past withdrawal fits), scores 1 when asked and answered, 0.6 when asked but not yet answered (asked before the patient trusted the doctor), and 0 with a critical error when not asked. No risk category is ever printed (NICE NG225).
Observation: expected MSE signs are those the patient visibly showed for at least 5 s, plus a tremor revealed by holding the hands out; recall earns 16 and precision 4, so recording signs that were not there costs marks, and signs never shown cost nothing.
Communication: six MIRS and Calgary-Cambridge items (introduction, confidentiality, share of open questions, repeated empathy, summarising, checking understanding) and the final trust, minus judgmental lines, real interruptions (at least 1 s of the reply cut off), jargon and stacked questions.
Feedback: a global rating (provisional cut-offs, capped by critical errors), the three highest-stakes changes with words to try, and comments quoting the learner's own lines.
AI examiner notes (formative, never scored): the local model rates eight verbal items zero-shot at temperature 0, and every quote is checked against the transcript; praise for a line the rules flag is not shown.

## 6. Discussion, limitations and ethics
Discussion (to write after results). Expected themes: whether an explicit patient-mind model beats a single rich prompt on fidelity; whether examiners find the live Twin view useful for marking and feedback; what the latency floor is on consumer hardware.
Limitations.
Four draft stations (depression, two acute-ward scenarios, alcohol dependence); psychosis, mania and anxiety are untested.
Clinical content, trust thresholds and the debrief's global-rating cut-offs are drafts; faculty validation and a borderline-regression standard set are pending.
The Twin is brain-inspired, not a neural simulation; region names are a metaphor and must not be read as neuroscience claims.
LLM judges are biased, and the judge is the same model that plays the patient; flags were hand-reviewed by the developer, so faculty ratings are needed for every headline claim.
The AI examiner notes rest on the literature's general findings (within one point of experts 67-87% of the time), not on agreement with our faculty; they never enter the score.
Voices are synthetic and designed from text; the designed voice can drift between sentences, and accent and emotional range are limited.
Exam router accuracy is optimistic: one author wrote the rules and the test set.
All tests ran on one high-end laptop; median latency (2.2 s) still misses the 1.2 s target, and weaker hardware will be slower.
Non-verbal communication by the learner is not assessed.
Ethics. No real patient data is stored on device; any real-data calibration (for example DAIC-WOZ or MIMIC notes) requires data-use agreements and ethics approval. Learner speech never becomes patient facts. Sensitive content (suicidal ideation) carries a content note and a debrief with teaching points. Synthetic voices are designed from text, not cloned from real people. Staged demonstrations use pre-scripted replies and must be disclosed as such in any presentation or publication.
