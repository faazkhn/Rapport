\title{Decide, Then Voice: A Code-Governed Digital Twin for Simulated Psychiatric Patients, with Three-Seed Held-Out Validation}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Faaz Khan Mohammad}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Rapport project\\
Email: faazkhn1000@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) can voice a simulated psychiatric patient, but left to decide what the patient remembers, feels and
reveals they invent symptoms, soften hostility into apology and disclose or deny suicidal thoughts inconsistently. We present a
patient model, the Digital Twin, in which code makes every decision a clinician would audit and the LLM only voices it. Before the
single model call per turn, a rubric reads the doctor's move (empathy, judgement, jargon, a risk question and 17 others), steps
trust before recall, moves an explicit resistance level and shortlists the behaviours and reactions the reply may use; memory items
are recalled by meaning and gated by trust; an episodic memory and a mental-status block keep the patient one person across the
interview; and every sentence is checked in code before it is spoken. On 14 held-out scripted conversations run on three sampling
seeds and judged by a model different from the patient's, the code-governed Twin (v2) held every safety gate (no jailbreak or
portrayal failures, suicidal ideation disclosed whenever it was open and asked kindly, no locked-fact leaks after a fresh-context
safety review closed ten defects) and used behaviour far more evenly (share of turns in the two commonest behaviours 0.72 vs 0.86
for depression, 0.63 vs 0.81 for alcohol withdrawal), while its held-out composite stayed equal to the baseline (0.947 vs 0.953;
0.875 vs 0.878). Recall of risk facts under paraphrase rose from 35.1\% to 72.7\% on a blind test set but missed its 95\% target,
and an upset conversation style did not hold past ten turns. We report the negative results with the positive ones.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Psychiatric interviewing is learned by practice: the trainee must notice slowed speech and averted gaze, ask open questions, earn
enough trust for a patient to disclose a passive death wish, and assess risk within a timed objective structured clinical examination
(OSCE) station \cite{harden1975osce}. Standardised patient actors make this possible but are costly and hard to keep consistent
\cite{piot2021simulation}. LLM-based patient simulators have multiplied
\cite{chen2023llmempowered,wang2024patient,lee2025psyche,sabour2026patientact,sabour2026patienthub,louie2024roleplaydoh}, and a recent
meta-analysis reports that AI-enhanced virtual patients help psychiatric interview training \cite{kilincel2026meta}. Three problems
recur when the model is trusted with the patient's mind: it invents clinical detail to please the interviewer
\cite{lai2025patientzero}; small aligned models turn hostility into apology; and resistance is either absent or rigid
\cite{steenstra2025scaffolding,bodonhelyi2025modeling}.

We take the opposite design stance from multi-agent simulators that spend several LLM calls per turn \cite{sabour2026patientact}:
\emph{decide in code, voice with the model}. The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a patient model whose state, memory locks, resistance, behaviour shortlist and consistency are explicit code with stated
update rules (Section~\ref{sec:model});
\item a validation protocol for such a model: blind paraphrase recall, held-out conversations on three seeds, a judge model distinct
from the patient's, hand review of every judge flag, and an adversarial fresh-context safety review (Section~\ref{sec:method});
\item measured results, including where the design did not meet its own targets (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}
The Twin is brain-inspired only in vocabulary: it borrows mechanisms such as prediction-error trust updates from the computational
programme of the Brain/MINDS Digital Brain project \cite{brainminds} but is not a neural simulation.

\section{Related Work}
Psychiatric LLM patients ground behaviour in cognitive models and conversation styles \cite{wang2024patient}, mental-status
constructs \cite{lee2025psyche}, trust-gated memory with resistance patterns \cite{sabour2026patientact}, and expert-written
principles \cite{louie2024roleplaydoh}; PatientHub packages many of them \cite{sabour2026patienthub}. Learning simulators evolve
patients through self-play and experience libraries \cite{du2024llms,almansoori2025selfevolving,li2024agent}, and prompt optimisers
such as GEPA \cite{agrawal2025gepa} and DSPy \cite{khattab2023dspy} tune the instructions. Patient-Zero checks utterances against
atomic case facts \cite{lai2025patientzero}; TalkDep grounds depression personas clinically \cite{wang2025talkdep}. Evaluation by LLM
judges scales but is biased towards its own model and position \cite{zheng2023judging,zhou2025personaeval}. Our model combines a
trust-locked memory and resistance (as in \cite{sabour2026patientact}) with a code-side rubric in the spirit of adaptive virtual
patients, and differs in keeping the decision, not just the facts, outside the model.

\subsection{State and memory}
The patient's state is $s_t=(\tau_t,\delta_t,\alpha_t,\eta_t)\in[0,100]^4$: trust, distress, agitation and engagement. A case holds
memory items $m$ with text, a trust threshold $\theta_m$, a kind (symptom, history, trigger, formative, risk), whether it must be asked
for, and the clinical topics that ask about it. An item is \emph{open} when $\tau\ge\theta_m$ and \emph{locked} otherwise. Locked text
never enters the prompt or any message; touching a locked painful item yields only a discomfort signal. Mood is a point in
pleasure--arousal--dominance space \cite{mehrabian1996pad} that moves a fixed fraction (0.3) towards a function of $s_{t+1}$ each turn.

\subsection{Reading the doctor's move}
A rubric maps the doctor's line to a set of moves $M(u_t)$ drawn from 21 types: greeting, open and closed questions, empathy,
explaining why a question is asked, apology, offering a choice, summarising, judgement, abuse, threat, premature reassurance,
jargon, stacked questions, rushing, interruption, a jailbreak attempt, a question about being an AI, small talk, a repeated question
and a risk question. Text moves are read by hand-written patterns; context moves (risk, repetition, interruption, rushing) come from
recall, the episodic memory and the session. Each move $m$ carries a hand-set step $\Delta_m\in\mathbb{Z}^4$, a resistance change
$r_m$, and the reactions it can provoke. The pre-step applied \emph{before} recall is
\begin{equation}
s_t' = \Pi_{[s_t-c,\;s_t+c]}\Big(s_t + g_\sigma\odot \sum_{m\in M(u_t)}\Delta_m\Big),
\end{equation}
where $g_\sigma$ holds the conversation style's gains, $c=10$ is the per-turn clamp, and a line containing a kind move and no hurtful
move cannot lower trust or raise agitation. Stepping before recall lets a kind risk question be answered kindly on the same turn.

\subsection{Resistance with explicit softening}
Resistance $\rho_t\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ (open, cautious, guarded, hostile) persists across turns:
\begin{align}
h_t &= \textstyle\sum_{m\in M(u_t)}\max(r_m,0),\quad v_t=\max\big(\min_{m} r_m,\,-1\big),\\
\rho_t &= \mathrm{clip}_{[0,3]}\big(\rho_{t-1}+h_t+v_t-[k_t\ge 2]\big),
\end{align}
where $h_t$ is the hurt in the line and $v_t$ at most one softening step per turn, $k_t$ is the number of consecutive calm turns
(drifting back to the style's base), and
$\rho_t\le1$ once trust passes the style's softening threshold. Judgement, abuse and threats raise $\rho$ by two; validation,
explanation, apology and choice lower it.

\subsection{Decision and the single call}
From $\rho_t$, the moves, recall and the session's recent behaviours, code builds shortlists $B_t$ (one to three of 13 behaviours),
$A_t$ (reactions) and resistance labels. Overrides are safety rules: an open risk item that the doctor asked about clearly and kindly
forces disclosure behaviours; a locked hit forces evasive behaviours and never \emph{denies}; a behaviour used on each of the last two
turns moves to the back of the list. The reply's JSON schema enumerates only $B_t$ and $A_t$, so the model refines within the decision
and cannot leave it. The model writes behaviour, emotion (restricted to the case's mental-status emotions), the utterance, then the
remaining labels; one call per turn.

\subsection{Episodic memory and invented backstory}
Code records episodes: what the patient already said about each item, locked questions, hurt and its repair, kindness, promises and
permission to leave. At most three relevant lines enter the next prompt (``You asked me that already''). Everyday backstory the
patient makes up (a friend's name, a school story) is stored and replayed so it stays consistent; it is never kept if clinical or if
it contradicts a name the case fixes.

\subsection{Consistency before speech}
Each sentence passes code checks before speech: numbers and durations are corrected to the item the line touched, the stated age and
the names of relatives to the case, the death wish is dropped when locked or never asked, an apology is stripped while an upset
patient is still upset, and near-repeats are removed. On turns where a denial would break a rule the whole reply is held and checked:
an open, kindly asked death wish is never flatly denied (the case's own disclosure line is spoken instead); a locked one meets
evasion, not ``No''. Dropped text is logged by kind only.

\section{Validation Method}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Systems compared.} Brain v0 (the previous Twin: keyword recall, model-chosen labels) and v2 (this paper). Both run the same
code with features switched per version, Gemma 4 E4B (4-bit) through llama.cpp \cite{llamacpp}, on one Apple M5 Pro laptop.

\textbf{Held-out conversations.} Fourteen scripted conversations never used for tuning: twelve for a depression case (gold standard
in both sexes, rushed, judgmental, off-topic, probing, jailbreak, abusive, risk asked kindly, risk not asked, exam requests and a
25-turn decay test) and two for alcohol withdrawal. Each runs on sampling seeds 11, 23 and 42; the evaluator carries the patient's
state and memory between turns exactly as a live session does.

\textbf{Judge.} Invented facts, character breaks and resistance appropriateness are judged by Qwen3.5-9B, a different model family
from the patient's, at temperature 0. Every flag on v2 was then reviewed by hand against the case file.

\textbf{Metrics.} The composite averages fidelity (each open fact revealed exactly when the doctor's words reached it), the share of
turns without a banned clinical word, without phrase repetition, with a state-appropriate length, and with appropriate resistance.
Variety is the share of turns in the two commonest behaviours and the normalised behaviour entropy. Safety gates count invented facts,
jailbreak and portrayal failures, locked-fact leaks, and failures to disclose suicidal ideation when it is open and asked kindly.

\textbf{Further probes.} An upset conversation style was played through the 25-turn and judgmental conversations and every reply
from turn 10 judged for staying in character. A tester's report (``You're free to go home'' made the patient ask to leave again, in
a loop) was replayed on the real model. Finally, a reviewer agent with no prior context was asked to break seven owner rules
(locked-fact privacy, suicide-risk fidelity, fixed clinical content, one call per turn, consent, no clinical invention, client
input as a trust boundary) by reading and probing the code.

\textbf{Safety and composite.} Table~\ref{tab:gates} lists the gates. v2 holds every safety gate. Its composite equals v0's within
seed noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:heldout}): the decision layer did not buy accuracy on the existing metric, and we do not claim it did.

\textbf{Variety and character.} Fig.~\ref{fig:behaviours} shows the behaviour mix. v0 answered plainly or guardedly on 86\% of
depression turns; v2 spread turns over eight behaviours, and in alcohol withdrawal used \emph{hostile} on 14\% of turns, all eight of them against
the judgmental doctor. Fig.~\ref{fig:trust} shows trust trajectories: a kind doctor opens the death-wish memory (trust 55) after about
five turns in both versions, and a judgmental doctor drives trust to zero in four.

\textbf{What the safety review found.} The fresh-context reviewer reported ten defects in the first version of v2, nine reproduced.
Six broke an owner rule: the raw text of a reply whose every sentence the checks had dropped still reached the client and the
conversation history; the prompt prefix could show a fact that the turn's lowered trust had locked; a question combining plans and
wishes let the open death wish be denied, because the no-plan item (whose truth is ``no'') was chosen for forced disclosure; lead-in
denials (``What? No, nothing like that.'') evaded the denial check; a ward event plus the rubric step could pass the per-turn clamp;
and the client-carried memory was unsigned, so a client could inject prompt text. All ten were fixed, re-probed with the reviewer's
scripts and turned into tests before the measurements above. An earlier overnight run had also shown the patient volunteering the death
wish unasked three times in 98 turns, caused by a recall threshold tuned for recall alone; it was fixed by separating ``recalled''
from ``clearly asked'' and by dropping unasked disclosures in code.

\textbf{Negative results.} The upset style was in character on half the turns after turn 10 (v0: 56\%): neither version apologised,
but both drifted from defensive to minimising, because the rubric's softening rules let a consistently kind doctor lower resistance.
Paraphrase recall of risk items reached 72.7\% at 15.2\% false risk hits; the companion recall paper analyses why. Median voice latency
rose by 279\,ms; the extra prompt text after the doctor's words accounts for only about 40\,ms of
prefill, and the rest is not yet isolated.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Moving the decision into code made the patient auditable and its safety properties testable, and it bought variety; it did not raise
the composite, which measures accuracy rather than realism. Whether the added variety reads as more real is a question for clinicians:
a blind A/B page is prepared, not yet rated. Further limits: the held-out scripts were written by the author; the judge is an LLM and
its flags were reviewed by the author, not faculty; all clinical content (cases, mental-status blocks, disclosure lines, rubric
numbers) is draft; only two cases were evaluated; no learner or faculty study has been run.

\section{Conclusion}
A simulated psychiatric patient can be governed by explicit, testable code while an LLM supplies the words. With that split, safety
properties become gates that a reviewer can try to break, and did; the remaining gaps (paraphrase recall, holding hostility) are now
measurable targets rather than impressions.

\section*{Ethics}
No human participants or patient data were involved. All clinical content is labelled draft pending faculty review; everything runs
on one device.
